The Number We Left Unexplained

Last letter ended with a number we did not explain. In 2024, while 1.17 million international patients came to Korea, only 7,147 came for cancer treatment — fewer than in 2019. The whole market more than doubled; that one segment shrank. We have since found the explanation, and it is not about Korean medicine. It is about distance. This letter is about what that means for anyone reading from far away.

Esther Yoon Secretary General

Medicine Is a Local Business

The preference for treatment close to home is stronger than most people assume — strong enough to override both money and quality.

Walmart once offered employees discounted surgery at top-tier hospitals in other US states, fully subsidised. Uptake fell far below projections, as the Wall Street Journal reported. Employees would not cross state lines for better care that cost them nothing.

Across borders the pattern holds. US CDC research shows Americans who seek affordable care abroad go to Mexico, Canada, or Costa Rica. Not across an ocean.

  When price and quality are comparable, proximity wins.

The Gulf Test

The cleanest test of this rule is the one place where cost is not a factor at all. Several Gulf states fund overseas treatment for serious illness in full — medical bills, flights, companion lodging, interpretation. These patients can go anywhere on earth.

Where do they go? Kuwaiti government figures: the UK 28%, the US 24%, Germany 23%. Saudi Arabia names the same three.

Even Asia’s celebrated hubs obey the rule. Singapore attracts roughly 650,000 medical travellers a year, Malaysia 1.08 million — but over half of Singapore’s and more than 60% of Malaysia’s come from neighbouring Indonesia.

Two things are visible here. The UK and Germany rank first and third because they are close — locality holds even for patients with unlimited budgets. And the United States, the most distant of the three, still takes 24%: distance can be overcome, but only by decades of institutional reputation and referral networks. That is the price of the exception. What reads as long-haul success is proximity in disguise.

Perception Decides More Than Capability

Korea’s two largest source countries show what actually drives the choice.

Japanese patients are Korea’s single largest group — and 69.7% of them see dermatology, 14.0% plastic surgery. There is no decisive clinical gap between the two countries, and Japan’s insurance system keeps costs low, so there is little reason to travel for treatment. Korea is a good-value option for aesthetics, not a treatment destination.

Chinese patients account for 22.3% of all arrivals and readily choose Korea for screening and cosmetic work. For cancer, they go to the US, Germany, or Japan. Not because Korean oncology has been measured and found wanting, but because the label “advanced medicine” sits elsewhere in their minds.

This is the uncomfortable part. Patients do not choose on verified capability. They choose on perception — reputation, referral, advertising. Which is worth remembering when a hospital looks reassuring to you: ask whether you are responding to a record or to an impression.

Where Travelling for Treatment Does Work

The data does show where treatment-driven travel to Korea works — and it confirms the same rule rather than breaking it.

Patients from Mongolia, Central Asia, and the Russian Far East come disproportionately for actual treatment. Among Mongolian patients, internal medicine (25.0%) and screening centres (11.5%) rank highest, and a Korea Tourism Organization survey found 62% of prospective Mongolian medical travellers preferred Korea. Central Asia shows internal medicine at 28.0% and screening at 19.3%. Kazakhstan ranked first among all nationalities for foreign cancer patients in 2024, third for cerebrovascular and cardiac disease, fourth for severe intractable conditions — growing 15–38% year on year in every category.

What these regions share is two conditions at once: a real capability gap at home, and Korea within reachable distance. That combination is what makes crossing a border rational.

So the question to ask yourself is not “is Korean medicine good.” It is: does my home system genuinely lack what I need, and is Korea close enough that the follow-up problem stays manageable? If both are true, you are in the same position as the patients who actually make this work. If only the first is true, the honest answer is that distance will cost you more than the quality gain returns — and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

One Addition to the Three Questions

Our first letter set out three things to confirm before choosing any hospital: the evidence it is registered to treat international patients, who you contact if something goes wrong afterwards, and whether the person guiding you is paid a commission.

For a treatment patient travelling a long distance, the second question changes weight. A cosmetic result shows within weeks. Serious illness unfolds across diagnosis, therapy, and monitoring — and most of that time you will be in your own country, where Korea’s rules do not permit remote consultation with your treating physician. So ask it in a harder form: not whether follow-up exists, but who specifically, through what channel, in what language, and what happens at month six. A hospital that has an answer has thought about patients like you. One that does not, has not.
