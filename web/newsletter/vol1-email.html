What “1.17 Million” Doesn’t Tell You About Your Choice

In 2024, 1.17 million foreign patients came to Korea. The following year, 2025, the number nearly doubled again, to 2.01 million. But these figures mean Korea’s healthcare “market” has grown — they are not information about the “hospital” you will choose. This piece is about what can fill the gap in between.

Esther Yoon Secretary General
Korea Medical Tourism Promotion Association

Market Numbers Are Not Decision Information

According to the attraction-performance analysis by the Ministry of Health and Welfare and the Korea Health Industry Development Institute, the 1.17 million foreign patients of 2024 represent a 93.2% increase over the previous year and more than double the 497,000 of 2019, just before the pandemic. Total treatment revenue nearly tripled over the same period, from 409.1 billion won to 1.2401 trillion won. Cumulative patients since the attraction program took off in 2009 have passed 5 million, and in 2024 alone, patients came from 202 countries.

The speed of the recovery is also unusual. In the same year, while inbound tourism barely returned to pre-pandemic levels, foreign patients from Japan, China, and Taiwan grew to two to three times their 2019 levels. Medicine came back before tourism, and faster.

That is as far as the press coverage goes. But to someone who has to choose a hospital, these numbers say nothing. That many people came, and that they were treated safely and went home, are separate propositions.

The Structure the Average Hides

The 1.17 million are not evenly distributed.

Specialties are concentrated. By patient count, dermatology accounted for 56.6% of the total (705,000 patients) and plastic surgery 11.4% — 68.0% between them. By revenue the share rises to 77.3%, meaning those two specialties generate more per patient. The countries with the highest year-over-year growth were Taiwan (550.6%), Japan (135.0%), and China (132.4%) — and dermatology drove those surges as well.

Geography is concentrated. 85.4% of patients (1 million) were treated in Seoul. Seoul’s share actually rose 21 percentage points from 64.4% in 2019.

So is the type of institution. 82.0% of foreign patients used clinic-level facilities. General hospitals (6.0%) and tertiary hospitals (5.1%) together account for less than 11%. The reality of the 1.17 million is closer to minor and cosmetic procedures at Seoul clinics than to severe-care treatment at large hospitals.

There is also an indicator pointing the other way. 7,147 foreign cancer patients came to Korea in 2024, still short of the 2019 level of roughly 11,000. While the market as a whole more than doubled, the severe-care segment alone shrank.

To sum up: if you are coming for a skin procedure, 1.17 million is roughly the number of people like you. But if you are coming for surgery or serious treatment, the market you belong to is far smaller — and shrinking. Depending on why you are coming, the same number means something entirely different.

The Low Scores Come from the Process, Not the Medicine

In surveys asking why patients chose Korean healthcare, medical technology and equipment and institutional reputation rank at the top. By contrast, only 12.7% cited “ease of use,” and just 3.8% cited “service infrastructure for foreign patients.” The quality of care is recognized; the process around it is not.

After returning home, things are more fragile still. 39.5% cited post-treatment recovery care as a difficulty, and 31.2% cited staying in contact with their medical team. Korea does not yet allow physician-patient telemedicine, so the institutional channel for continuity of care after going home is narrow. A procedure takes days, but recovery takes weeks, and side effects can appear months later. Most of that time, the patient is outside Korea.

Industry diagnoses point to the same spot: the axis of competition has moved from price and speed to trust and aftercare. A domain has emerged that “cheap and fast” no longer explains.

The Records Already Exist. They Are Just Hard to Reach

Here is a less-known fact: the 1.17 million statistic itself comes from records. It is compiled from performance reports submitted by institutions registered under the Act on Support for Overseas Expansion of Healthcare and Attraction of International Patients — roughly 3,500 medical institutions and 2,100 facilitator agencies.

In other words, which hospitals are registered to receive foreign patients, and which agencies are registered facilitators, exists as public record. The problem is that those records are scattered, hard to reach in foreign languages, and patients have no good way to tell a hospital’s marketing copy from the public record. The information is not missing — the distance between the information and the patient is long.

So MediCheck Does Exactly One Thing

MediCheck’s job is to close that distance. We do not rate hospitals; we record two things: which public record a hospital’s listing is based on, and the date we verified that record. Users can open the original record themselves and check that our entry is correct.

  There is one principle: we do not write a sentence we cannot verify.

That is why this is not a list of “good hospitals” but a list of “what the record says.”

We Also Record What We Don’t Do

We do not sell ranking. The list sorts two ways only — by name and by date of last update — and we built no path for manually placing an institution at the top. The moment we take advertising money to reorder the list, it becomes the same “marketing copy” we criticized.

We do not assign grades. A grading system is a proposal under review, and no institution currently carries a grade. Attaching grades before verifiable criteria exist would be doing things in the wrong order.

We do not answer inquiries on hospitals’ behalf. A user’s inquiry is read and answered by the hospital directly. MediCheck records only when it was forwarded and when the response came. The moment we intervene in the content of an answer, the structure makes us the guarantor of its quality — and that is not an area we can take responsibility for.

Wherever You Look for a Hospital, Check These Three Things

You do not have to use our list. But whatever route you take to find a hospital, we recommend checking three things before you decide.

ONE What is the evidence that this hospital is registered to receive foreign patients, and can I open that record myself?
TWO If something goes wrong after the procedure, whom do I contact, and through what channel — especially after I have returned home?
THREE Who is the person guiding me, and do they receive a commission from the hospital?

The third question may feel unfamiliar. But Korea caps referral commissions for foreign patients by law (clinics 30% · hospitals 20% · tertiary hospitals 15%). That a facilitator registration system exists and that commission caps are written into law means this is not a question you may ask — it is a question you should ask.

Making the answers to these three questions visible on a single screen — that is all MediCheck does.
